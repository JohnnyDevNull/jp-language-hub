Cases answer the question "what job does this phrase have in the sentence?"
Learn that job first; article endings and pronoun forms make much more sense
afterward.

## Choose by role

| Role or question | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| I need the whole decision map | [Cases as roles](./cases-as-roles/) |
| who or what does the action? | [Nominative](./nominative/) |
| who or what is directly affected? | [Accusative](./accusative/) |
| to whom, for whom, or where with many location patterns? | [Dative](./dative/) |
| possession, formal style, or fixed written patterns | [Genitive](./genitive/) |

## What to learn first

Start with [cases as roles](./cases-as-roles/) before memorizing endings. Then
use the detail pages when you need one case in isolation.

- [Nominative](./nominative/) is the subject case.
- [Accusative](./accusative/) is the direct-object and motion-into case in many
  common patterns.
- [Dative](./dative/) covers recipients, many indirect objects, and many
  location phrases.
- [Genitive](./genitive/) is less common in speech, but important for formal
  writing and fixed expressions.

## Where cases show up

- [Prepositions and case](../prepositions/prepositions-and-case/) shows which
  small words force a case.
- [Articles and gender](../nouns-articles/articles-and-gender/) shows how case
  becomes visible in *der*, *den*, *dem*, *ein*, and adjective endings.
- [Pronoun case choice](../pronouns/pronoun-case-choice/) shows the same roles
  on forms like *ich*, *mich*, and *mir*.
