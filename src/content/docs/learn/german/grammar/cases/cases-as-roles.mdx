<KeyTakeaway>
- Nominative names the subject: who or what does the action.
- Accusative marks the directly affected object or direction with two-way prepositions.
- Dative often marks the recipient, beneficiary, location, or fixed preposition pattern.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

German cases are not decorative endings. They show what job a noun phrase is doing in the sentence.

## Use this now

| Ask | Choose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who does it? | nominative | <span lang="de">Der Hund schläft.</span> |
| Who or what is affected? | accusative | <span lang="de">Ich sehe den Hund.</span> |
| To whom or for whom? | dative | <span lang="de">Ich gebe dem Hund Wasser.</span> |
| Whose? | genitive | <span lang="de">Das ist das Haus des Mannes.</span> |

## Core rule

First decide the role, then choose the article or pronoun form that signals that role. Case is the sentence job; endings are the visible clue.

The role can come from the verb, a preposition, or a noun relationship. For
example, <span lang="de">helfen</span> selects dative:
<span lang="de">Ich helfe dem Mann.</span> A two-way preposition selects by
meaning: <span lang="de">in den Park</span> is a destination, while
<span lang="de">im Park</span> is a location. Do not choose a case from an
English “to” or “for” alone.



<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Ich helfe den Mann."
	correct="Ich helfe dem Mann."
	lang="de"
>
	Some verbs, including <span lang="de">helfen</span>, require dative even when English uses a direct object.
</MistakeBox>

<MemoryTip>
Ask the role question first: doer, affected thing, receiver/location, or owner.
</MemoryTip>
