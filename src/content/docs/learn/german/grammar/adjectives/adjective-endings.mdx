Choose German adjective endings from article signal, gender, number, and case.

## Use this now

Use this four-step check before you look at a table:

1. Is the adjective before the noun?
2. Which case does the noun phrase have?
3. Does the article already show gender, number, and case clearly?
4. Add the adjective ending that completes the missing signal.

## Core rule

After a strong article signal, adjectives usually use weak endings; without a strong signal, the adjective carries more information.

| Noun phrase type | Signal logic | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definite article | Article carries the strong signal; adjective usually uses `-e` or `-en`. | <span lang="de">der gute Kaffee</span>, <span lang="de">den guten Kaffee</span> |
| Indefinite article | The article sometimes lacks the signal; adjective fills the gap. | <span lang="de">ein guter Kaffee</span>, <span lang="de">ein gutes Buch</span> |
| No article | The adjective carries most of the signal. | <span lang="de">guter Kaffee</span>, <span lang="de">frisches Wasser</span> |

Predicate adjectives do not take these endings: <span lang="de">Der Kaffee ist gut</span>, not <span lang="de">*Der Kaffee ist guter</span>. The ending belongs to the adjective inside the noun phrase.

## Signal sharing

The useful mental model is not "three tables first"; it is signal sharing. German wants the noun phrase to show case, gender, and number somewhere. If the article already shows the signal, the adjective can be lighter. If the article is missing or ambiguous, the adjective must do more work.
