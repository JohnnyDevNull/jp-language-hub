<KeyTakeaway>
- Some prepositions always take accusative, some always take dative, and some choose by meaning.
- Two-way prepositions use accusative for direction and dative for location.
- Learn frequent preposition phrases as chunks, not word-for-word translations.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

Prepositions are where case becomes practical. The small word often decides the case of the whole phrase.

## Use this now

| Preposition type | Case | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fixed accusative | accusative | <span lang="de">durch den Park</span> |
| fixed dative | dative | <span lang="de">mit dem Bus</span> |
| two-way direction | accusative | <span lang="de">in den Park gehen</span> |
| two-way location | dative | <span lang="de">im Park sein</span> |

## Core rule

When a preposition belongs to a phrase, choose the case from the preposition pattern before you think about the noun phrase in isolation.

Separate two decisions: first identify the preposition pattern, then inflect
the noun phrase. A phrase such as <span lang="de">mit dem neuen Bus</span>
contains a fixed dative preposition, while <span lang="de">in den neuen Park</span>
uses accusative because it expresses a destination. Verb- or adjective-led
combinations are collected on [Dependent prepositions](../dependent-prepositions/).



<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Ich gehe mit den Bus."
	correct="Ich gehe mit dem Bus."

>
	Do not translate English “with the” mechanically; <span lang="de">mit</span> requires dative.
</MistakeBox>
