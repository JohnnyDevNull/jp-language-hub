<KeyTakeaway>
- A finished action with a finished time expression takes the past simple, not
  the present perfect: *I saw him yesterday.*
- An ongoing situation that started in the past takes the present perfect:
  *I have lived here for three years.*
- English keeps the subject before the verb after a fronted element, and puts
  a frequency adverb before the main verb.
</KeyTakeaway>

German and English share enough structure that the differences hide well.
These are the ones that persist into fluent speech.

## since and for

<LanguageComparison title="Duration up to now" languages={['de', 'en']}>
	<p slot="de">Ich wohne seit drei Jahren hier.</p>
	<p slot="en">I have lived here for three years.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German uses the present tense with *seit* for both a duration and a starting
point. English needs the present perfect, plus *for* for a stretch of time and
*since* for a point in time: *since 2020*, *for three years*.

## become and get

<MistakeBox incorrect="I become 30 next month." correct="I turn 30 next month." lang="en">
	German *bekommen* means **get** or **receive**, and *werden* means
	**become**. *Become* is only for changing into something else: *she became a
	doctor*. For ages, use *turn* or *be*; for receiving, use *get*.
</MistakeBox>

## Words that only look English

| German | Not English | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Handy | *a handy* | a mobile phone, a cell phone |
| Beamer | *a beamer* | a projector |
| Chef | *my chef* | my boss |
| eventuell | *eventually* | possibly, maybe |
| aktuell | *actually* | current, up to date |

*Eventually* means *in the end*, and *actually* means *in fact* — both are the
opposite of what a German speaker usually intends.

## information and other uncountables

<MistakeBox incorrect="I need more informations." correct="I need more information." lang="en">
	*Information* has no plural. The same applies to *advice*, *furniture*,
	*equipment*, *news* (singular despite the *-s*), and *knowledge*. Count them
	with a phrase: *two pieces of information*, *a piece of advice*.
</MistakeBox>

If you are unsure whether a noun can be pluralized, quantified, or used with
*a/an*, continue with
[countable and uncountable nouns](../../grammar/nouns-articles/countable-and-uncountable-nouns/).

## Articles: diagnose before you guess

<MistakeBox incorrect="I have car." correct="I have a car." lang="en">
	A singular count noun normally needs an article or another determiner. For
	missing *a/an*, overused *the*, and generic meanings like *Dogs need
	exercise*, use [Articles](../../grammar/nouns-articles/articles/).
</MistakeBox>

## make and do

| German | Not English | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ein Foto machen | *make a photo* | take a photo |
| Hausaufgaben machen | *make homework* | do your homework |
| einen Kurs machen | *make a course* | take a course |
| Sport machen | *make sport* | do sport, exercise |
| einen Fehler machen | — | make a mistake |

## Adverb placement

<MistakeBox incorrect="I go often to the cinema." correct="I often go to the cinema." lang="en">
	A frequency adverb goes before the main verb, but after *be* and after an
	auxiliary: *she is often late*, *I have often wondered*. German puts it in
	the middle field, which lands after the verb.
</MistakeBox>

## get to know

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="I have got to know her last year."
	correct="I got to know her last year."
	lang="en"
>
	Two errors at once: *kennenlernen* is *get to know* or *meet*, and *last
	year* is a finished time, so the past simple is required. *I have got to* on
	its own means *I must*.
</MistakeBox>

## Present perfect for a finished past

<LanguageComparison title="Finished action, finished time" languages={['de', 'en']}>
	<p slot="de">Ich habe ihn gestern gesehen.</p>
	<p slot="en">I saw him yesterday.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German uses the *Perfekt* for the spoken past regardless of the time
expression. English blocks the present perfect as soon as the time is over:
*yesterday*, *last week*, *in 2019*, *when I was a child*.
