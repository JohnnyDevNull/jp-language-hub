<KeyTakeaway>
- A neutral English statement normally follows **subject + verb + object + place
  + time**.
- English main clauses are stricter than German: a fronted time or place phrase
  does not normally move the verb before the subject.
- Save inversion for questions, negatives with special opening words, and a few
  formal patterns.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

English sounds natural when the main sentence frame stays stable. German lets
you move a time or place phrase to the front and then put the verb second;
English normally keeps the subject before the verb.

## Use this now

1. Start with the person or thing the sentence is about.
2. Add the finite verb.
3. Add the object or complement.
4. Put place before time when both are simple adverbials.
5. If time or place goes first, keep **subject + verb** after it.

Use [questions and do-support](../questions-and-do-support/) when the sentence
is a question. Use [adverb placement](../adverb-placement/) when the problem is
a word like *often*, *always*, *still*, or *already*.

## Core rule

The default English statement pattern is:

> **subject + finite verb + object/complement + place + time**

The opening position is optional. When it is filled, it adds emphasis or
orientation, but it normally does not change the order of subject and verb.

## What changes from German

German main clauses use V2: the finite verb stays in the second constituent
position. English statements do not follow that rule.

<LanguageComparison title="Time expression first">
	<p slot="de">Gestern habe ich Sarah am Bahnhof getroffen.</p>
	<p slot="en">Yesterday, I met Sarah at the station.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Igår träffade jag Sarah på stationen.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German and Swedish put the finite verb after the fronted time phrase. English
keeps **I met**, not *met I*. This is one of the first word-order habits German
speakers need to switch off in English.

## Place before time

When both place and time come after the verb phrase, English usually prefers
place before time:

- <span lang="en">We had dinner <strong>at a small restaurant</strong> <strong>last night</strong>.</span>
- <span lang="en">She works <strong>from home</strong> <strong>on Fridays</strong>.</span>
- <span lang="en">They arrived <strong>in London</strong> <strong>at six</strong>.</span>

This is a useful default, not a law. Put a time phrase earlier when it is the
main contrast:

<p lang="en">On Fridays, she works from home.</p>

The important point is that the fronted phrase still does not trigger German
V2 order.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Yesterday met I Sarah at the station."
	correct="Yesterday, I met Sarah at the station."
	lang="en"
>
	Do not copy German main-clause inversion after a fronted time or place
	expression. In an English statement, keep the subject before the verb.
</MistakeBox>

<MemoryTip>
After a fronted time or place phrase, ask: **Who did it?** Put that subject
next, then the verb.
</MemoryTip>
