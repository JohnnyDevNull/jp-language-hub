- Frequency adverbs usually go before the main verb:
  <span lang="en">I often work from home.</span>
- With <span lang="en">be</span>, put the adverb after
  <span lang="en">am</span>, <span lang="en">is</span>, or
  <span lang="en">are</span>: <span lang="en">She is often late.</span>

  <span lang="en">I have often wondered.</span>

English frequency adverbs have a small but strict position rule. For
German-speaking learners, the main trap is copying the German middle field and
placing the adverb after the main verb.

## Use this now

Ask what kind of first verb the sentence has:

| Sentence type | Put the frequency adverb | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One main verb | before the main verb | <span lang="en">I usually drink tea.</span> |
| <span lang="en">be</span> as the verb | after <span lang="en">be</span> | <span lang="en">She is usually calm.</span> |
| Auxiliary + main verb | after the first auxiliary | <span lang="en">They have usually finished by five.</span> |

This page is mostly about frequency adverbs such as *always*, *usually*,
*often*, *sometimes*, *rarely*, *hardly ever*, and *never*. It also covers the
common lookup words *already*, *still*, and *yet*.

## Core rule

Frequency adverbs sit near the verb phrase, but their exact position depends on
what comes first in that verb phrase.

The first auxiliary carries the grammar of the sentence. The adverb comes after
that first auxiliary, before the main meaning verb.

## Main verb: before the verb

If the sentence has only one main verb, put the frequency adverb before it:

- <span lang="en">I often go to the cinema.</span>
- <span lang="en">She usually starts at nine.</span>
- <span lang="en">We never eat lunch at our desks.</span>

This is the pattern you will use most often in the
[simple present](../../verbs/simple-present/), because routines and habits often
need frequency words.

## be: after the verb

When <span lang="en">be</span> is the verb, put the frequency adverb after it:

- <span lang="en">He is always friendly.</span>
- <span lang="en">They are often tired after work.</span>
- <span lang="en">I am usually at home on Sundays.</span>

Do not move the adverb in front of <span lang="en">be</span> unless you are
using a marked style that this page does not need.

## Auxiliary: after the first auxiliary

When the verb phrase starts with an auxiliary, put the frequency adverb after
the first auxiliary:

- <span lang="en">I have often thought about it.</span>
- <span lang="en">She has never visited London.</span>
- <span lang="en">You can usually find a seat here.</span>
- <span lang="en">They will probably call tomorrow.</span>

In the [present perfect](../../verbs/present-perfect/), this means the adverb
comes between <span lang="en">have</span> or <span lang="en">has</span> and
the past participle.

## already, still, and yet

These words are not frequency adverbs, but they cause the same German-transfer
problem because German can place the equivalent word later in the clause.

| Word | Usual position | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *already* | before the main verb or at the end | <span lang="en">I have already sent it.</span> |
| *still* | before the main verb, after *be* | <span lang="en">She still works here.</span> |
| *yet* | usually at the end in questions and negatives | <span lang="en">Have you sent it yet?</span> |

With the present perfect, *already* usually comes between the auxiliary and the
participle:

<p lang="en">I have already finished.</p>

In negative sentences, *yet* normally goes at the end:

<p lang="en">I have not finished yet.</p>

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="Habit with one main verb" languages={['de', 'en']}>
	<p slot="de">Ich gehe oft ins Kino.</p>
	<p slot="en">I often go to the cinema.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German can put <span lang="de">oft</span> after the finite verb. English
usually puts <span lang="en">often</span> before the main verb.

<LanguageComparison title="With be" languages={['de', 'en']}>
	<p slot="de">Sie ist oft zu spät.</p>
	<p slot="en">She is often late.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

Here German and English look more similar because English
<span lang="en">be</span> keeps the adverb after the verb.

## Common mistake

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="I go often to the cinema."
	correct="I often go to the cinema."
	lang="en"
>
	German word order makes this position feel natural, but English usually
	puts a frequency adverb before the main verb. Only move it after the verb
	when that verb is <span lang="en">be</span> or when it follows the first
	auxiliary.
</MistakeBox>

<MemoryTip>
Find the first verb. If it is <span lang="en">be</span> or an auxiliary, put
the adverb after it. If it is the main verb, put the adverb before it.
</MemoryTip>
