## Use this now

| Meaning you need | Choose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Facts, routines, habits | [simple present](../simple-present/) | <span lang="en">I work from home.</span> |
| Happening now or temporary | [present progressive](../present-progressive/) | <span lang="en">I am working from home this week.</span> |
| Finished past time | [simple past](../simple-past/) | <span lang="en">I worked there in 2022.</span> |
| Past connected to now | [present perfect](../present-perfect/) | <span lang="en">I have worked here for three years.</span> |
| Decision, promise, prediction | [will future](../will-future/) | <span lang="en">I will call you later.</span> |
| Plan or evidence | [going to future](../going-to-future/) | <span lang="en">I am going to call her.</span> |
| Future arrangement | [present progressive](../present-progressive/) | <span lang="en">I am meeting her tomorrow.</span> |
| Timetable or schedule | [simple present](../simple-present/) | <span lang="en">The train leaves at 8.</span> |
| In progress at a future time | [future progressive](../future-progressive/) | <span lang="en">I will be working at 8.</span> |
| Completed before a future point | [future perfect](../future-perfect/) | <span lang="en">I will have finished by Friday.</span> |

## Core rule

English time forms do not only place an action in the past, present, or future.
They also show how the speaker sees the action:

- **simple** forms often present the action as a fact, habit, completed event,
  decision, prediction, or scheduled item
- **progressive** forms show an action as ongoing, temporary, arranged, or in
  progress at a specific time
- **perfect** forms connect one time to another: past to now, or earlier future
  to later future

That is why a German present or perfect cannot be copied mechanically into
English. Start with the question the listener needs answered.

## Present decisions

Choose [simple present](../simple-present/) for stable facts, routines, and
states:

- <span lang="en">She lives in Hamburg.</span>
- <span lang="en">I usually start at nine.</span>
- <span lang="en">This button opens the menu.</span>

Choose [present progressive](../present-progressive/) for something happening
now, around now, temporarily, or changing:

- <span lang="en">She is staying in Hamburg this week.</span>
- <span lang="en">I am starting at eight today.</span>
- <span lang="en">The company is growing.</span>

Open [simple present vs present progressive](../simple-present-vs-present-progressive/)
when both forms look possible.

## Past decisions

Choose [simple past](../simple-past/) when the past time is finished:

- <span lang="en">I saw him yesterday.</span>
- <span lang="en">We launched the project in 2024.</span>

Choose [present perfect](../present-perfect/) when the past is still connected
to now: experience, present result, unfinished time, or duration up to now.

- <span lang="en">I have seen that film.</span>
- <span lang="en">We have worked here since 2021.</span>

Open [simple past vs present perfect](../simple-past-vs-present-perfect/) when
you are deciding between a finished past frame and a past-to-now frame.

## Future decisions

English does not have one default future form. The best choice depends on the
type of future meaning:

- Use [will future](../will-future/) for decisions made now, promises, offers,
  and many predictions.
- Use [going to future](../going-to-future/) for intentions and predictions
  based on present evidence.
- Use [present progressive](../present-progressive/) for arranged future
  events with another person or fixed plan.
- Use [simple present](../simple-present/) for timetables, programmes, and
  schedules.
- Use [future progressive](../future-progressive/) for an action that will be
  in progress at a future time.
- Use [future perfect](../future-perfect/) for something completed before a
  future deadline or reference point.
