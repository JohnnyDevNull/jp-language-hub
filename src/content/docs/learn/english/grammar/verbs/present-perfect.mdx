<KeyTakeaway>
- Form the present perfect with **have** or **has** plus the **past participle**:
  *I have seen it*, *she has finished*.
- Use it when a past action clearly reaches into now: experience, unfinished
  time, duration up to now, or a result you want to foreground.
- Do not use it with finished-time expressions such as *yesterday*, *last
  year*, or *in 2020*.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

The present perfect connects a past action to the present. For German-speaking
learners, the hard part is not the form but the boundary: German *Perfekt* is
often just a spoken past tense, while English present perfect normally keeps a
link to now.

## Use this now

Choose the present perfect when the sentence answers one of these questions:

| Question | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Life experience? | <span lang="en">I have visited London.</span> |
| Result visible now? | <span lang="en">She has lost her keys.</span> |
| Still inside the time period? | <span lang="en">We have had three meetings this week.</span> |
| Situation continuing until now? | <span lang="en">He has worked here since 2021.</span> |

Choose the simple past when the time is finished: <span lang="en">I visited
London last year.</span>

## Core rule

Use **have** with <span lang="en">I</span>, <span lang="en">you</span>,
<span lang="en">we</span>, and <span lang="en">they</span>. Use **has** with
<span lang="en">he</span>, <span lang="en">she</span>, and
<span lang="en">it</span>.

| Subject | Auxiliary | Past participle | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I / you / we / they | have | worked | <span lang="en">We have worked.</span> |
| he / she / it | has | worked | <span lang="en">She has worked.</span> |

The past participle is often the regular **-ed** form:
<span lang="en">worked</span>, <span lang="en">finished</span>,
<span lang="en">visited</span>. Many common verbs are irregular:
<span lang="en">seen</span>, <span lang="en">done</span>,
<span lang="en">gone</span>, <span lang="en">written</span>,
<span lang="en">been</span>.

## Experience

Use the present perfect for experience when the exact past time is not the
point.

- <span lang="en">I have never tried sushi.</span>
- <span lang="en">Have you ever worked abroad?</span>
- <span lang="en">She has been to Canada twice.</span>

The hidden meaning is "up to now in my life." If you add a finished time, move
to the simple past: <span lang="en">I tried sushi last week.</span>

## Result now

Use the present perfect when you want the past action to explain the present
situation. In American English, the simple past is also common with recent
results, especially with words like *already* and *just*.

- <span lang="en">I have lost my phone.</span> The phone is missing now.
- <span lang="en">The train has arrived.</span> The train is here now.
- <span lang="en">She has finished the report.</span> The report is ready now.

This is why *already*, *just*, and *yet* often appear with the present perfect:
<span lang="en">I have just sent it.</span> Treat this as a strong default, not
as a claim that the simple past is impossible in every English variety.

## Unfinished time

Use the present perfect when the time period is still open.

- <span lang="en">I have had two coffees today.</span> Today is not over.
- <span lang="en">We have met three times this week.</span> This week is still
  happening.
- <span lang="en">She has called me twice this morning.</span> The morning is
  still open.

If the time period is finished, use the simple past:
<span lang="en">We met three times last week.</span>

## Duration up to now: since and for

Use the present perfect for a situation that started in the past and is still
true now.

| Use | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| starting point | since + point in time | <span lang="en">I have lived here since 2021.</span> |
| length of time | for + duration | <span lang="en">I have lived here for three years.</span> |

Do not copy German *seit* with an English present tense.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="Finished time blocks the present perfect" languages={['de', 'en']}>
	<p slot="de">Ich habe ihn gestern gesehen.</p>
	<p slot="en">I saw him yesterday.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German can use *Perfekt* with <span lang="de">gestern</span>. English cannot:
*yesterday* names a finished time, so the present perfect no longer fits.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="I have seen him yesterday."
	correct="I saw him yesterday."
	lang="en"
>
	German *Perfekt* often translates to English simple past. Before choosing
	*have/has + participle*, check whether the sentence names a finished time:
	*yesterday*, *last year*, *in 2020*, *when I was a child*.
</MistakeBox>

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="I live here since 2021."
	correct="I have lived here since 2021."
	lang="en"
>
	German uses the present tense with <span lang="de">seit</span>. English uses
	the present perfect because the living started in the past and continues
	now.
</MistakeBox>
