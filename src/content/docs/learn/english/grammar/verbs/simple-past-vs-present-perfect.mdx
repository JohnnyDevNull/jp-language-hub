<KeyTakeaway>
- Use the simple past when the action belongs to a finished past time: *I saw her yesterday.*
- Use the present perfect when you want to foreground the link to now:
  *I have lost my key.*
- German *Perfekt* often becomes English simple past when the time is finished.

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

German often uses *Perfekt* for normal spoken past. English asks a different
question first: is the past time finished, or is there a connection to now?

## Use this now

| If the sentence means... | Choose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a finished time: *yesterday*, *last week*, *in 2020*, *when I was a child* | simple past | <span lang="en">I met him yesterday.</span> |
| experience before now, no finished time | present perfect | <span lang="en">I have met him before.</span> |
| a result you want to connect to now | present perfect | <span lang="en">I have lost my passport.</span> |
| an unfinished time period: *today*, *this week*, *so far* | present perfect | <span lang="en">I have sent three emails today.</span> |
| duration up to now with *for* or *since* | present perfect | <span lang="en">I have lived here since 2021.</span> |

## Core rule

The simple past places an event in the past and closes it there. The present
perfect links a past event or state to the present.

- <span lang="en">I lived in Berlin in 2020.</span> The year is finished, so
  use the simple past.
- <span lang="en">I have lived in Berlin since 2020.</span> The situation
  started in the past and continues now, so use the present perfect.

## Finished past time

Use the simple past when the time expression is over. The English sentence can
still be relevant now, but the grammar follows the finished time marker.

- <span lang="en">I watched the film last night.</span>
- <span lang="en">She called me two minutes ago.</span>
- <span lang="en">We visited London in 2019.</span>

This is the main German transfer trap: *Ich habe den Film gestern gesehen*
looks like a perfect form, but English needs *I saw the film yesterday*.

## Connected to now

Use the present perfect when the past is still connected to the present, or
when you want to make that connection explicit. American English often allows
the simple past for recent results where British English strongly prefers the
present perfect.

| Function | Example | Why present perfect? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| experience | <span lang="en">I have been to Scotland.</span> | The life experience matters, not the finished date. |
| result now | <span lang="en">She has broken her arm.</span> | The result is visible or important now. |
| unfinished time | <span lang="en">We have had three meetings this week.</span> | *This week* is still open. |
| duration up to now | <span lang="en">He has worked here for five years.</span> | The state reaches the present. |

With duration, German uses present tense with *seit*: <span lang="de">Ich wohne
seit 2021 hier.</span> English uses present perfect: <span lang="en">I have
lived here since 2021.</span>

<MemoryTip>
Ask for the time anchor first: closed past time means simple past; open time
means present perfect. For a present result, present perfect highlights the
link to now, but the simple past may also be possible in American English.
</MemoryTip>
