<KeyTakeaway>
- German gender does not predict <Sv>en</Sv> or <Sv>ett</Sv>; the two systems
  are unrelated.
- Swedish inflects the predicative adjective where German leaves it bare:
  <Sv>Huset är stort.</Sv>
- <Sv>inte</Sv> follows the finite verb in a main clause, where German *nicht*
  drifts to the end.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

German is the best available bridge to Swedish, which is exactly why its
patterns slip through unnoticed. Each entry links to the rule that fixes it.

## Guessing en/ett from German gender

<MistakeBox incorrect="ett bok, en brev, en bord" correct="en bok, ett brev, ett bord" lang="sv">
	*das Buch* is <Sv>en bok</Sv>, *der Brief* is <Sv>ett brev</Sv>, *der Tisch*
	is <Sv>ett bord</Sv>. Learn the gender with the noun, and use the ending
	signals instead of German. See
	[en/ett](../../grammar/nouns-articles/en-ett/).
</MistakeBox>

## Leaving the predicative adjective bare

German inflects an adjective before a noun but not after *sein*. Swedish
inflects it in both positions:

| German | Swedish |
| --- | --- |
| ein großes Haus | <Sv>ett stort hus</Sv> |
| Das Haus ist groß. | <Sv>Huset är stort.</Sv> |
| Die Häuser sind groß. | <Sv>Husen är stora.</Sv> |

See [adjective agreement](../../grammar/adjectives-adverbs/agreement/).

## sin instead of hans/hennes

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Anna såg Erik och hans hund. (if the dog is Anna's)"
	correct="Anna såg Erik och sin hund."
	lang="sv"
>
	Use <Sv>sin</Sv>, <Sv>sitt</Sv>, <Sv>sina</Sv> when the owner is the subject
	of the same clause; <Sv>hans</Sv> and <Sv>hennes</Sv> point at someone else.
	German *sein* covers both, so the distinction has to be made consciously. See
	[sin, sitt, sina](../../grammar/pronouns-function-words/sin-sitt-sina/).
</MistakeBox>

## German subordinate word order

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Jag stannar hemma eftersom jag idag inte arbetar."
	correct="Jag stannar hemma eftersom jag inte arbetar idag."
	lang="sv"
>
	German sends the verb to the end of the subordinate clause. Swedish keeps
	subject, then <Sv>inte</Sv>, then the verb. See
	[BIFF](../../grammar/sentence-structure/biff/).
</MistakeBox>

## Looking for the missing case system

Swedish nouns have no case. Do not compensate with extra prepositions or
article forms:

| German | Swedish |
| --- | --- |
| Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch. | <Sv>Jag ger mannen boken.</Sv> |
| das Buch des Mannes | <Sv>mannens bok</Sv> |
| mit dem Auto | <Sv>med bilen</Sv> |

Only pronouns still inflect: <Sv>med mig</Sv>, <Sv>till honom</Sv>, never
*med jag*.

## Assuming German verb particles

German separable prefixes travel to the end of the clause. A Swedish loose
particle stays right behind the verb, and only <Sv>inte</Sv> gets in between:

| German | Swedish |
| --- | --- |
| Ich stehe um sieben auf. | <Sv>Jag går upp klockan sju.</Sv> |
| Ich mag Fisch nicht. | <Sv>Jag tycker inte om fisk.</Sv> |

See [particle verbs](../../grammar/verbs/particle-verbs/).

## Putting inte where nicht goes

<MistakeBox incorrect="Jag kommer idag inte." correct="Jag kommer inte idag." lang="sv">
	In a main clause <Sv>inte</Sv> comes directly after the finite verb, before
	adverbials and most objects. See
	[placement of inte](../../grammar/sentence-structure/inte-placement/).
	For *kein* use <Sv>ingen</Sv>, <Sv>inget</Sv>, <Sv>inga</Sv>:
	<Sv>Jag har ingen bil.</Sv>
</MistakeBox>
