<KeyTakeaway>
- Every noun is an en-word or an ett-word, so learn it as
  <span lang="sv">en bil</span> or <span lang="sv">ett hus</span>, never on its own.
- If you have to guess, guess <span lang="sv">en</span>: most nouns are
  en-words, and many word endings tell you the gender.
- A compound word takes the gender of its **last** part:
  <span lang="sv">ett sommarhus</span>, because <span lang="sv">ett hus</span>.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

Every Swedish noun belongs to one of two groups, called its gender
([<span lang="sv">genus</span>](../../../glossary/#genus)). An **en-word** takes
the article <span lang="sv">en</span> (<span lang="sv">en bil</span>, a car), an
**ett-word** takes <span lang="sv">ett</span> (<span lang="sv">ett hus</span>, a
house). German has three such groups — <span lang="de">der</span>,
<span lang="de">die</span>, <span lang="de">das</span> — English has none. You need
the gender for every noun you use, because the article, the ending for "the" and
the adjective all depend on it.

## Use this now

When you meet a new noun, go through this list from the top:

| Situation | What to do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You are learning a new noun | Learn it with its article, not alone. | <span lang="sv">en bil</span>, <span lang="sv">ett hus</span> |
| You have no clue | Guess <span lang="sv">en</span>, then check later. | <span lang="sv">en dag</span>, <span lang="sv">en fråga</span> |
| The noun has a typical ending | Let the ending guide you, but still learn the noun with its article. | <span lang="sv">en tidning</span>, <span lang="sv">ett bibliotek</span> |
| The noun is a compound word | Take the gender of its last part, then check words you do not know. | <span lang="sv">ett sommarhus</span> from <span lang="sv">ett hus</span> |
| You add an adjective | Match the adjective to the noun's gender and number. | <span lang="sv">en stor bil</span>, <span lang="sv">ett stort hus</span> |

The most useful habit is simple: say every new noun aloud with its article.
That one habit gives you the article, the form for "the", and the right
adjective ending later.

## Core rule

| Gender | Indefinite singular | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| en-word | <span lang="sv">en bil</span> | a car |
| ett-word | <span lang="sv">ett hus</span> | a house |

No rule tells you the gender of every Swedish noun. Learn the article together
with the noun: not just <span lang="sv">bil</span>, but
<span lang="sv">en bil</span>. Some word endings are reliable enough to help
you guess, and compound words follow their last part.

<RuleBox>
	Learn a Swedish noun together with its article. The article shows the gender,
	and the gender decides the other forms: the ending for "the" and the
	adjective.
</RuleBox>

## Endings that point to a gender

Most Swedish nouns are en-words, so <span lang="sv">en</span> is the better guess
when you have nothing else to go on. Many word endings make the guess safer.

These endings are normally en-words:

| Ending | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| <span lang="sv">-a</span> | <span lang="sv">en flicka</span>, <span lang="sv">en gata</span>, <span lang="sv">en vecka</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-are</span> | <span lang="sv">en lärare</span>, <span lang="sv">en bagare</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-else</span> | <span lang="sv">en händelse</span>, <span lang="sv">en rörelse</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-het</span> | <span lang="sv">en möjlighet</span>, <span lang="sv">en nyhet</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-ning</span>, <span lang="sv">-ing</span> | <span lang="sv">en tidning</span>, <span lang="sv">en övning</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-dom</span> | <span lang="sv">en sjukdom</span>, <span lang="sv">en visdom</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-lek</span> | <span lang="sv">en kärlek</span>, <span lang="sv">en storlek</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-nad</span> | <span lang="sv">en byggnad</span>, <span lang="sv">en skillnad</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-tion</span>, <span lang="sv">-sion</span> | <span lang="sv">en station</span>, <span lang="sv">en diskussion</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-ist</span>, <span lang="sv">-ent</span>, <span lang="sv">-ör</span> | <span lang="sv">en turist</span>, <span lang="sv">en student</span>, <span lang="sv">en frisör</span> |

These endings are normally ett-words:

| Ending | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| <span lang="sv">-ande</span>, <span lang="sv">-ende</span> | <span lang="sv">ett meddelande</span>, <span lang="sv">ett leende</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-eri</span> | <span lang="sv">ett bageri</span>, <span lang="sv">ett tryckeri</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-um</span> | <span lang="sv">ett museum</span>, <span lang="sv">ett centrum</span>, <span lang="sv">ett faktum</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-ment</span> | <span lang="sv">ett dokument</span>, <span lang="sv">ett argument</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-tek</span> | <span lang="sv">ett bibliotek</span>, <span lang="sv">ett apotek</span> |
| <span lang="sv">-gram</span> | <span lang="sv">ett program</span>, <span lang="sv">ett diagram</span> |

Three cases need care:

- <span lang="sv">-a</span> has a small group of ett-words:
  <span lang="sv">ett öga</span>, <span lang="sv">ett öra</span>,
  <span lang="sv">ett hjärta</span>.
- <span lang="sv">-ande</span> and <span lang="sv">-ende</span> are ett-words
  when they name an action, but en-words when they name a person:
  <span lang="sv">ett meddelande</span> against
  <span lang="sv">en studerande</span>.
- <span lang="sv">-skap</span> is split. Words for things or groups tend to be
  ett-words (<span lang="sv">ett landskap</span>,
  <span lang="sv">ett sällskap</span>), while words for relationships and
  qualities tend to be en-words (<span lang="sv">en vänskap</span>,
  <span lang="sv">en kunskap</span>). Check this ending rather than guessing.

An ending such as <span lang="sv">-e</span> gives no useful signal at all:
compare <span lang="sv">en pojke</span> with
<span lang="sv">ett äpple</span>.

Nouns for people and animals are usually en-words —
<span lang="sv">en man</span>, <span lang="sv">en kvinna</span>,
<span lang="sv">en hund</span> — but not always:
<span lang="sv">ett barn</span>, <span lang="sv">ett djur</span>,
<span lang="sv">ett lejon</span>.

## Compound words follow their last part

Swedish joins words together all the time: <span lang="sv">sommar</span> +
<span lang="sv">hus</span> makes <span lang="sv">sommarhus</span>. That makes this
the most useful gender rule: the last part of the compound
([<span lang="sv">efterled</span>](../../../glossary/#efterled)) decides the
gender. The parts in front of it do not matter:

| Parts | Compound |
| --- | --- |
| <span lang="sv">en cykel</span> + <span lang="sv">ett hjul</span> | <span lang="sv">ett cykelhjul</span> |
| <span lang="sv">ett tåg</span> + <span lang="sv">en station</span> | <span lang="sv">en tågstation</span> |
| <span lang="sv">en sommar</span> + <span lang="sv">ett hus</span> | <span lang="sv">ett sommarhus</span> |
| <span lang="sv">en post</span> + <span lang="sv">ett kontor</span> | <span lang="sv">ett postkontor</span> |

The form for "the" and the plural usually follow the last part too:
<span lang="sv">ett cykelhjul → cykelhjulet</span>,
<span lang="sv">en tågstation → tågstationen</span>.

<RuleBox title="Rule of thumb">
	For a compound word, look at the last part. Its gender, its ending for "the"
	and its plural usually carry over to the whole word.
</RuleBox>

## "A car" and "the car"

For "a", Swedish puts the article in front: <span lang="sv">en bil</span>. For
"the", it adds an ending to the noun instead:

| Gender | Indefinite | Definite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| en-word | <span lang="sv">en bil</span> | <span lang="sv">bilen</span> |
| ett-word | <span lang="sv">ett hus</span> | <span lang="sv">huset</span> |

## Adjective agreement

An adjective changes its ending to match the noun: en-word or ett-word, one or
more. The article shows you which one you have:

- <span lang="sv">en <strong>stor</strong> bil</span> — a big car
- <span lang="sv">ett <strong>stort</strong> hus</span> — a big house
- <span lang="sv"><strong>stora</strong> bilar</span> — big cars
- <span lang="sv"><strong>stora</strong> hus</span> — big houses

The basic pattern: the **plain form** with an en-word, usually **-t** with an
ett-word, usually **-a** in the plural. Some adjectives have irregular forms; the
full explanation is on [adjective agreement](../../adjectives-adverbs/agreement/).

## "The big car": two markers for "the"

When an adjective stands before a noun with "the", Swedish marks "the" twice:
with a separate word in front (<span lang="sv">den</span>) and with the ending on
the noun (<span lang="sv">-en</span>):

<p lang="sv">den stora bilen</p>

This is called
[double definiteness](../../../glossary/#dubbel-bestämdhet). See
[adjective agreement](../../adjectives-adverbs/agreement/#definite-noun-phrases-and-double-definiteness)
for the whole pattern and the exception after words like <span lang="sv">min</span>
(my).

## Plural

You cannot tell the plural ending from <span lang="sv">en</span> or
<span lang="sv">ett</span> alone:

- <span lang="sv">en bil → bilar</span>
- <span lang="sv">ett hus → hus</span>
- <span lang="sv">ett äpple → äpplen</span>

Learn the important plural forms with the noun. See the
[plural overview](../plural-overview/).

<MistakeBox title="Common mistakes">
What a thing is does not decide <span lang="sv">en</span> or
<span lang="sv">ett</span>, and <span lang="sv">en</span> does not work for every
word. Do not take the
gender of a compound from its first element either:
<span lang="sv">❌ en cykelhjul</span>, <span lang="sv">✅ ett cykelhjul</span>.

<span lang="sv">❌ en hus</span>  
<span lang="sv">✅ ett hus</span>

Also make the adjective match:

<span lang="sv">❌ ett stor hus</span>  
<span lang="sv">✅ ett stort hus</span>
</MistakeBox>

<MemoryTip>
	Treat the article as part of the word: learn
	<span lang="sv"><strong>en bil</strong></span> and
	<span lang="sv"><strong>ett hus</strong></span>. Add the four noun forms when
	you can: <span lang="sv">en bil, bilen, bilar, bilarna</span>.
</MemoryTip>
