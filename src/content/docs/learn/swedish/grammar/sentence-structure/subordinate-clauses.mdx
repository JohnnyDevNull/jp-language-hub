<KeyTakeaway>
- A subordinate clause is part of a larger sentence and often begins with a
  subordinator such as <Sv>att</Sv>, <Sv>eftersom</Sv>, <Sv>om</Sv>, or <Sv>när</Sv>.
- Its practical core frame is **subordinator + subject + sentence adverb +
  finite verb + rest**.
- If the whole subordinate clause comes first, the following main clause uses
  inversion.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

A subordinate clause adds content such as what someone says, why something
happens, a condition, or a time. It cannot normally stand alone with that same
function: <Sv>eftersom jag är trött</Sv> needs a main clause around it.

## Use this now

| Meaning | Start the subordinate clause with… | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| that / reported content | <Sv>att</Sv> | <Sv>Jag vet att hon kommer.</Sv> |
| because | <Sv>eftersom</Sv> | <Sv>Jag stannar eftersom jag är trött.</Sv> |
| if / whether | <Sv>om</Sv> | <Sv>Vi går ut om det inte regnar.</Sv> |
| when | <Sv>när</Sv> | <Sv>Ring mig när du är klar.</Sv> |
| before | <Sv>innan</Sv> | <Sv>Ät innan du går.</Sv> |

## Core rule

Use this reliable frame when the subordinate clause contains a sentence adverb
such as <Sv>inte</Sv>:

<SentenceSchema
	caption="The Swedish subordinate-clause frame"
	rowHeader="Meaning"
	columns={['Subordinator', 'Subject', 'Sentence adverb', 'Finite verb', 'Non-finite verb', 'Rest']}
	rows={[
		{ label: 'Reason', cells: ['eftersom', 'jag', 'inte', 'kan', 'komma', 'idag'] },
		{ label: 'Content', cells: ['att', 'hon', 'redan', 'har', 'köpt', 'biljetten'] },
		{ label: 'Condition', cells: ['om', 'det', 'inte', 'regnar', '', ''] },
	]}
/>

Without a sentence adverb, the subject is followed directly by the finite verb:

- <Sv>Jag vet **att hon kommer**.</Sv>
- <Sv>Ring mig **när du är klar**.</Sv>

The [BIFF rule](../biff/) owns the narrower question of why <Sv>inte</Sv> and
other sentence adverbs precede the finite verb here.

## When the subordinate clause comes first

The whole subordinate clause can fill the first field of a following main
clause. The main clause then places its finite verb before its subject:

<SentenceSchema
	caption="A subordinate clause before a main clause"
	rowHeader="Clause"
	columns={['First field', 'Finite verb', 'Subject', 'Rest']}
	rows={[
		{
			label: 'Subordinate + main',
			cells: ['När mötet är slut', 'går', 'vi', 'hem.'],
		},
	]}
/>

That main-clause shift belongs to [Inversion](../inversion/), not to the internal
order of the subordinate clause itself.

## Indirect questions

An indirect question also behaves as a subordinate clause:

- <Sv>Jag vet inte **var hon bor**.</Sv>
- <Sv>Han frågade **om tåget var sent**.</Sv>

[Question words](../../questions-negation/question-words/) owns the lexical choices
and the direct-versus-indirect question pattern.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="Because-clause">
	<p slot="de">… weil ich heute nicht kommen kann.</p>
	<p slot="en">… because I cannot come today.</p>
	<p slot="sv">… eftersom jag inte kan komma idag.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German also marks subordinate clauses with special word order, but sends the
finite verb to the end. Swedish keeps the finite verb after the sentence
adverb and before any non-finite verb.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Jag stannar hemma eftersom jag kan inte komma."
	correct="Jag stannar hemma eftersom jag inte kan komma."
	lang="sv"
>
Do not carry main-clause sentence-adverb placement into a subordinate clause.
</MistakeBox>

<MemoryTip>
Build the dependent block from left to right: **link word → subject → sentence
adverb → finite verb → the rest**.
</MemoryTip>
