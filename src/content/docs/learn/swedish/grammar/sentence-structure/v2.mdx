<KeyTakeaway>
- In a Swedish statement, the conjugated verb is always the second sentence
  part — not necessarily the second word.
- If the sentence starts with something other than the subject, the subject
  moves behind the verb: <span lang="sv">Idag köper jag en bil.</span>
- Only the conjugated verb moves to second place. Other verb forms, such as
  <span lang="sv">köpa</span> or <span lang="sv">köpt</span>, stay further back.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

In a Swedish statement, the conjugated verb
([<span lang="sv">finit verb</span>](../../../glossary/#finit-verb)) — the verb
that shows the tense, like <span lang="sv">köper</span> or
<span lang="sv">har</span> — always comes second. Before it stands exactly one
sentence part: a word or a group of words that belongs together, such as
<span lang="sv">jag</span>, <span lang="sv">idag</span> or
<span lang="sv">efter jobbet</span>. So you say
<span lang="sv">Jag köper en bil idag</span>, but also
<span lang="sv">Idag köper jag en bil</span>. You use this rule in almost every
Swedish sentence; German works the same way, English does not.

## Core rule

> In a Swedish statement, the **conjugated verb** comes **second**, after
> exactly one sentence part.

Think of the sentence as a row of boxes. The first box holds one sentence part,
however many words it has. The second box always holds the conjugated verb:

The column names are the Swedish grammar terms. The first box is called
[<span lang="sv">fundament</span>](../../../glossary/#fundament), the
foundation of the sentence.

In a normal sentence the subject itself fills the first box, so box three stays
empty. As soon as something else comes first, the subject moves behind the verb:

<ExampleBox title="A time word in the first box" lang="sv">
	<p><strong>Idag</strong> <strong>köper</strong> jag en bil.</p>
</ExampleBox>

## When the first part has several words

One sentence part can be several words long:

<p lang="sv"><strong>Efter jobbet</strong> <strong>köper</strong> jag mat.</p>

<span lang="sv">Efter jobbet</span> is two words, but one sentence part: together
they say when. So <span lang="sv">köper</span> is still in second place, even
though it is the third word.

## Sentences with more than one verb

Only the conjugated verb moves to second place:

<p lang="sv">Idag <strong>vill</strong> jag köpa en bil.</p>
<p lang="sv">Idag <strong>har</strong> jag köpt en bil.</p>

<span lang="sv">köpa</span> (the basic form) and <span lang="sv">köpt</span> (the
form after <span lang="sv">har</span>) show no tense, so they stay further back.
In the schema they stand under
[<span lang="sv">infinit verb</span>](../../../glossary/#infinit-verb).

## What can come first?

Almost any sentence part:

- the subject: <span lang="sv">Jag arbetar hemma idag.</span>
- a time: <span lang="sv">Idag arbetar jag hemma.</span>
- a place: <span lang="sv">Hemma arbetar jag bäst.</span>
- an object: <span lang="sv">Den boken har jag redan läst.</span>
- a whole part-sentence: <span lang="sv">När jag är klar går jag hem.</span>

Whatever comes first gets a little more weight. The verb stays in second place
either way.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="Normal order">
	<p slot="de">Ich kaufe heute ein Auto.</p>
	<p slot="en">I am buying a car today.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Jag köper en bil idag.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

<LanguageComparison title="Time first">
	<p slot="de">Heute kaufe ich ein Auto.</p>
	<p slot="en">Today I am buying a car.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Idag köper jag en bil.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German works the same way: in a main clause the conjugated verb comes second.
English does not. After <span lang="en">today</span>, the subject still comes
before the verb.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="På måndag morgon jag börjar mitt nya jobb."
	correct="På måndag morgon börjar jag mitt nya jobb."
	lang="sv"
>
Count sentence parts, not words. When something other than the subject comes
first, the subject goes behind the verb.
</MistakeBox>

<SpecialRule language="Swedish" title="Where the rule applies">
	The rule covers statements. A yes/no question starts with the verb:
	<span lang="sv">Köper du en bil?</span> A question with a question word keeps
	the verb second: <span lang="sv">Vad köper du?</span> In a subordinate
	clause — a part-sentence that starts with a word like <span lang="sv">att</span>
	or <span lang="sv">eftersom</span> — the order changes again. See
	[Negation in subordinate clauses (BIFF)](../biff/).
</SpecialRule>

<MemoryTip>
First part → **conjugated verb** → subject (if it was not first) → the rest.
</MemoryTip>
