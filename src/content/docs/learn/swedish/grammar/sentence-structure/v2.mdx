<KeyTakeaway>
- The finite verb is the second **constituent**, not the second word.
- Whatever fills position one, the finite verb follows it; the subject then
  moves behind the verb.
- Only the finite verb counts. Non-finite verbs stay later in the clause.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

**V2** means that the finite verb normally occupies the second position in a
Swedish declarative main clause. It is one of the most important rules for
producing natural Swedish sentences, and German speakers already know it.

## Core rule

> In a Swedish declarative main clause, the **finite verb** normally comes in
> the **second constituent position**.

Read the clause as a row of fields. Position one is a single field no matter how
many words it holds, and the finite verb owns position two:

/>

In a neutral sentence the subject itself fills position one, so position three
stays empty. As soon as something else is fronted, the subject moves behind the
verb:

<ExampleBox title="Time expression in position one" lang="sv">
	<p><strong>Idag</strong> <strong>köper</strong> jag en bil.</p>
</ExampleBox>

## Position does not mean word

One constituent may contain several words:

<p lang="sv"><strong>Efter jobbet</strong> <strong>köper</strong> jag mat.</p>

<Sv>Efter jobbet</Sv> is one adverbial constituent. The verb <Sv>köper</Sv> is
still in the second sentence position even though it is the third written word.

## Compound verb phrases

V2 applies to the **finite** verb, not to every verb in the clause:

<p lang="sv">Idag <strong>vill</strong> jag köpa en bil.</p>
<p lang="sv">Idag <strong>har</strong> jag köpt en bil.</p>

The non-finite verbs <Sv>köpa</Sv> and <Sv>köpt</Sv> stay later in the clause.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="Neutral order">
	<p slot="de">Ich kaufe heute ein Auto.</p>
	<p slot="en">I am buying a car today.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Jag köper en bil idag.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

<LanguageComparison title="Time expression first">
	<p slot="de">Heute kaufe ich ein Auto.</p>
	<p slot="en">Today I am buying a car.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Idag köper jag en bil.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German is a useful bridge because its main clauses also normally follow V2.
English does not generally invert the subject and verb after an initial time
expression.

## What can occupy position one?

Common choices include:

- the subject: <Sv>Jag arbetar hemma idag.</Sv>
- time: <Sv>Idag arbetar jag hemma.</Sv>
- place: <Sv>Hemma arbetar jag bäst.</Sv>
- an object: <Sv>Den boken har jag redan läst.</Sv>
- a subordinate clause: <Sv>När jag är klar går jag hem.</Sv>

Changing position one changes the emphasis, not the V2 requirement.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="På måndag morgon jag börjar mitt nya jobb."
	correct="På måndag morgon börjar jag mitt nya jobb."
	lang="sv"
>
Do not count individual words and do not leave the subject before the verb after
fronting another constituent.
</MistakeBox>

<SpecialRule language="Swedish" title="Scope of V2">
	V2 describes declarative **main clauses**. A subordinate clause has a
	different internal pattern; in particular, sentence adverbs such as
	<Sv>inte</Sv> normally come before the finite verb. See [BIFF](../biff/).
</SpecialRule>

<MemoryTip>
Think in blocks: **first block → finite verb → subject (if it was not first) →
the rest**.
</MemoryTip>
