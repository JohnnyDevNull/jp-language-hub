<KeyTakeaway>
- The neutral main clause runs subject, finite verb, sentence adverb, remaining
  verb parts, object, adverbial.
- A sentence position holds one **constituent**, which may be several words.
- Swedish verbs do not change according to person, so the position of the verb
  carries the grammar instead.
</KeyTakeaway>

Swedish word order carries grammatical meaning. The most useful starting point is
the neutral main-clause pattern, but that pattern changes when another sentence
element is placed first or when the clause is subordinate.

## Core rule

In a neutral declarative main clause, Swedish usually follows this order:

> **subject + finite verb + sentence adverb + remaining verb parts + object + adverbial**

Read the clause as a row of fields. This is the neutral schema that
[V2](../v2/), [inversion](../inversion/) and [BIFF](../biff/) vary:

In a neutral clause the subject fills position one itself, so position three
stays empty. The German row fills the same opening fields, which is why the
neutral pattern feels familiar; German differs later in the clause, where it
sends non-finite verb forms to the very end.

- <Sv>Jag</Sv> — subject
- <Sv>köper</Sv> — finite verb
- <Sv>inte</Sv> — sentence adverb
- <Sv>bilen</Sv> — object
- <Sv>idag</Sv> — time adverbial

The finite verb carries tense and anchors the clause structure. Swedish verbs
do not change according to person:

- <Sv>Jag arbetar.</Sv> — I work.
- <Sv>Vi arbetar.</Sv> — We work.

## A constituent can contain several words

A sentence position is occupied by a **constituent**, not necessarily one word.

<p lang="sv">På måndag morgon börjar jag mitt nya jobb.</p>

The entire phrase <Sv>på måndag morgon</Sv> occupies the first position. The
finite verb <Sv>börjar</Sv> is therefore second and the subject follows it.

## Questions

A yes/no question normally begins with the finite verb:

<p lang="sv">Köper du bilen idag?</p>

A question-word question normally begins with the question word or phrase,
followed by the finite verb:

<p lang="sv">När köper du bilen?</p>

The question phrase <Sv>när</Sv> occupies the first position, so the finite verb
remains second.

## Main and subordinate clauses

The same fields also show what changes in a subordinate clause. Position two
stays empty there: the finite verb leaves it and follows the sentence adverb.

The [BIFF rule](../biff/) is a useful way to remember this subordinate-clause
pattern.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="Neutral statement">
	<p slot="de">Ich kaufe heute ein Auto.</p>
	<p slot="en">I am buying a car today.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Jag köper en bil idag.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

<LanguageComparison title="Time expression first">
	<p slot="de">Heute kaufe ich ein Auto.</p>
	<p slot="en">Today I am buying a car.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Idag köper jag en bil.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

The three languages can look similar in a neutral sentence. The important
difference appears when the first constituent changes: Swedish and German use
[V2 word order](../v2/), whereas English normally keeps the subject before the
verb.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="Idag jag köper en bil."
	correct="Idag köper jag en bil."
	lang="sv"
>
Do not copy English subject–verb order after a fronted Swedish adverbial.
</MistakeBox>

<MemoryTip>
Start with **who + does what**, then add the rest. If something else moves to
the front of a main clause, keep the finite verb in position two and move the
subject behind it.
</MemoryTip>
