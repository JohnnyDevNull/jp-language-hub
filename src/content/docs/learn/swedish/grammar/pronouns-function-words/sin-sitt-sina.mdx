<KeyTakeaway>
- Use <Sv>sin</Sv>, <Sv>sitt</Sv>, or <Sv>sina</Sv> when the possessive refers
  back to the third-person subject of the same clause.
- <Sv>hans</Sv>, <Sv>hennes</Sv>, and <Sv>deras</Sv> point to somebody other
  than that subject.
- The form follows the possessed noun: <Sv>sin jacka</Sv>, <Sv>sitt hus</Sv>,
  <Sv>sina vänner</Sv>.
</KeyTakeaway>

Swedish uses <Sv>sin</Sv>, <Sv>sitt</Sv>, and
<Sv>sina</Sv> when a third-person subject possesses something
and the possessive refers back to that same subject.

## Core rule

The form agrees with the thing possessed—not with the possessor:

| Possessed noun | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singular <Sv>en</Sv> noun | <Sv>sin</Sv> | <Sv>Sara tar sin jacka.</Sv> |
| Singular <Sv>ett</Sv> noun | <Sv>sitt</Sv> | <Sv>Sara säljer sitt hus.</Sv> |
| Plural noun | <Sv>sina</Sv> | <Sv>Sara träffar sina vänner.</Sv> |

In each sentence, Sara owns the jacket, house, or friends. Her gender does not
choose the form; the noun's gender or number does.

<MemoryTip title="Two questions">
First ask: “Does the possessive refer back to the third-person subject of this
clause?” If yes, then ask: “Is the possessed noun en, ett, or plural?”
</MemoryTip>

## Own or somebody else's?

The contrast with <Sv>hans</Sv>, <Sv>hennes</Sv>,
or <Sv>deras</Sv> can change who owns the object:

| Swedish | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| <Sv>Anna tog sin jacka.</Sv> | Anna took her own jacket. |
| <Sv>Anna tog hennes jacka.</Sv> | Anna took another woman's jacket. |
| <Sv>Erik tog sin jacka.</Sv> | Erik took his own jacket. |
| <Sv>Erik tog hans jacka.</Sv> | Erik took another man's jacket. |
| <Sv>De tog sina jackor.</Sv> | They took their own jackets. |
| <Sv>De tog deras jackor.</Sv> | They took another group's jackets. |

Context must identify the other person or group when
<Sv>hans</Sv>, <Sv>hennes</Sv>, or
<Sv>deras</Sv> is used.

## The clause boundary matters

The relevant subject is the subject of the same clause:

<Sv>Anna sa att Erik tog sin jacka.</Sv>

In the subordinate clause, <Sv>Erik</Sv> is the subject, so
<Sv>sin jacka</Sv> normally means Erik's own jacket—not Anna's.

Compare:

<Sv>Anna sa att Erik tog hennes jacka.</Sv>

Here <Sv>hennes</Sv> can refer to Anna or another woman, as
determined by context.

## Where sin, sitt, and sina do not apply

They are third-person reflexive possessives. First- and second-person subjects
use their ordinary possessives:

- <Sv>Jag tar min jacka.</Sv>
- <Sv>Du tar din jacka.</Sv>
- <Sv>Vi tar våra jackor.</Sv>

They also do not normally appear inside the subject itself:

- <Sv>Hans bil är ny.</Sv> — His car is new.
- <Sv>Hennes vänner kommer.</Sv> — Her friends are coming.

## Direct comparison

German and English do not encode the same own-versus-other distinction in the
possessive form. The same German and English sentence covers both readings,
while Swedish chooses:

<LanguageComparison title="Anna's own jacket">
	<p slot="de">Anna nimmt ihre Jacke.</p>
	<p slot="en">Anna takes her jacket.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Anna tar sin jacka.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

<LanguageComparison title="Another woman's jacket">
	<p slot="de">Anna nimmt ihre Jacke.</p>
	<p slot="en">Anna takes her jacket.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Anna tar hennes jacka.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

The German and English sentences can be ambiguous without context. Swedish
normally uses <Sv>sin</Sv> to make “her own” explicit.

## Common mistakes

<MistakeBox title="Common mistakes">
	- ❌ <Sv>Anna säljer hennes hus.</Sv> when Anna sells her own house
	  ✅ <Sv>Anna säljer sitt hus.</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Erik träffar sin vänner.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Erik träffar sina vänner.</Sv>
	- ❌ Choosing <Sv>sin</Sv> because the possessor is one person.
	  Choose by the possessed noun: <Sv>sin bil</Sv>,
	  <Sv>sitt hus</Sv>, <Sv>sina böcker</Sv>.
</MistakeBox>
