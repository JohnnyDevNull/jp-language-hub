<KeyTakeaway>
- <Sv>Som</Sv> is invariable and covers people and things, as subject and as
  object.
- A preposition never stands before <Sv>som</Sv>; it stays at the end of the
  relative clause.
- <Sv>Vilket</Sv> refers back to a whole clause, and <Sv>vars</Sv> is the
  formal *whose*.
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

Swedish relative clauses are easier than German ones: the relative pronoun does
not change with gender, number, or case. In almost every everyday sentence the
word is <Sv>som</Sv>.

## som is the default

<Sv>Som</Sv> is invariable. It works for people and things, and
as both subject and object of the relative clause:

- <Sv>Mannen som bor här heter Erik.</Sv> — *The man who lives
  here is called Erik.*
- <Sv>Boken som jag läste var bra.</Sv> — *The book that I read
  was good.*
- <Sv>Barnen som vi träffade var trevliga.</Sv> — *The children
  we met were nice.*

Compare the German equivalents, which change form:
<span lang="de">der Mann, **der** hier wohnt</span> against
<span lang="de">das Buch, **das** ich gelesen habe</span>. Swedish keeps
<Sv>som</Sv> in both.

## The preposition goes to the end

This is the rule that German speakers need most. <Sv>Som</Sv>
can never follow a preposition. The preposition stays behind in the relative
clause, at the end:

<LanguageComparison title="In the house">
	<p slot="de">das Haus, in dem ich wohne</p>
	<p slot="en">the house I live in</p>
	<p slot="sv">huset som jag bor i</p>
</LanguageComparison>

<LanguageComparison title="For the bus">
	<p slot="de">der Bus, auf den wir warten</p>
	<p slot="en">the bus we are waiting for</p>
	<p slot="sv">bussen som vi väntar på</p>
</LanguageComparison>

<LanguageComparison title="With the person">
	<p slot="de">die Person, mit der ich gesprochen habe</p>
	<p slot="en">the person I talked to</p>
	<p slot="sv">personen som jag talade med</p>
</LanguageComparison>

English puts the preposition at the end in the same way; German moves it to the
front with the relative pronoun.

<RuleBox title="Rule">
	Never write a preposition directly before <Sv>som</Sv>. Put it
	at the end of the relative clause instead:
	<Sv>❌ huset i som jag bor</Sv>,
	<Sv>✅ huset som jag bor i</Sv>.
</RuleBox>

## som can be dropped as an object

When <Sv>som</Sv> is the **object** of the relative clause, it
may be left out, just as English can drop *that*:

- <Sv>Boken (som) jag läste var bra.</Sv>

When it is the **subject**, it must stay:

- <Sv>Mannen som bor här</Sv> — not
  <Sv>❌ Mannen bor här</Sv>, which is a different sentence
  entirely.

## vilket refers to a whole clause

<Sv>Vilken / vilket / vilka</Sv> is the inflected relative
pronoun. In modern everyday Swedish you mainly need one of its uses:
<Sv>vilket</Sv> referring back to an entire preceding clause,
where English uses *which*:

- <Sv>Han kom för sent, vilket irriterade alla.</Sv> — *He
  arrived late, which annoyed everyone.*
- <Sv>Det regnade hela veckan, vilket vi inte hade väntat oss.</Sv>

Note that German normally uses <span lang="de">was</span> here, not a form of
<span lang="de">welcher</span>.

After a preposition, <Sv>vilken</Sv> is the form that is
grammatically possible, but the result is formal and written:
<Sv>ett problem för vilket det inte finns någon lösning</Sv>. In
speech, restructure the sentence instead.

## vars for "whose"

<Sv>Vars</Sv> is the genitive relative pronoun. It is
invariable, takes no article after it, and belongs to a somewhat formal
register:

- <Sv>en författare vars böcker jag älskar</Sv> — *an author
  whose books I love*

With a plural antecedent, formal Swedish uses <Sv>vilkas</Sv>.
In everyday speech, people usually avoid both and rebuild the sentence with
<Sv>som</Sv> and a possessive.

## där and dit for places

For a place, <Sv>där</Sv> often replaces
<Sv>som … i</Sv>, and <Sv>dit</Sv> covers
direction:

- <Sv>staden där jag bor</Sv> = <Sv>staden som jag
  bor i</Sv>
- <Sv>staden dit jag flyttade</Sv> — *the city I moved to*

## Word order inside the relative clause

A relative clause is a subordinate clause, so it follows the subordinate-clause
pattern: <Sv>inte</Sv> and similar adverbs come **before** the
verb.

- <Sv>Mannen som inte bor här</Sv>
- <Sv>Boken som jag aldrig läste</Sv>

See [BIFF and subordinate clauses](../../sentence-structure/biff/).

<MemoryTip>
Two sentences carry most of what you need:
<Sv><strong>Bussen som vi väntar på är sen.</strong></Sv> —
preposition at the end. <Sv><strong>Han kom sent, vilket
irriterade alla.</strong></Sv> — <Sv>vilket</Sv> for a whole
clause.
</MemoryTip>

## Common mistakes

<MistakeBox title="Common mistakes">
	- ❌ <Sv>huset i som jag bor</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>huset som jag bor i</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Mannen vilken bor här</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Mannen som bor här</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Han kom sent, som irriterade alla.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Han kom sent, vilket irriterade alla.</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Mannen som bor inte här</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Mannen som inte bor här</Sv>
	- Do not inflect <Sv>som</Sv> for gender or case the way German
	  inflects <span lang="de">der / die / das</span>.
</MistakeBox>
