Most Swedish adverbs are simply the ett-form of an adjective. That makes the
formation easy — the harder parts are a handful of irregular adverbs, the
position of sentence adverbs, and the pairs that distinguish place from
direction.

## Adverbs from adjectives

<RuleBox title="Rule">
	The adverb is the adjective's <Sv>-t</Sv> form: the same form
	you already use with an ett-word.
</RuleBox>

| Adjective | Adverb | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>{snabb.morphology.en}</Sv> | <Sv>{snabb.morphology.ett}</Sv> | <Sv>Han springer snabbt.</Sv> |
| <Sv>tydlig</Sv> | <Sv>tydligt</Sv> | <Sv>Hon talar tydligt.</Sv> |
| <Sv>vacker</Sv> | <Sv>vackert</Sv> | <Sv>Hon sjunger vackert.</Sv> |
| <Sv>försiktig</Sv> | <Sv>försiktigt</Sv> | <Sv>Kör försiktigt!</Sv> |

Because the adverb reuses the ett-form, the spelling patterns are the same
ones. See
[adjective agreement](../agreement/#spelling-patterns).

English marks the difference with *-ly* and German not at all, so the Swedish
system sits between the two: there is an ending, but it is one you already
know.

	<p slot="de">Er läuft schnell.</p>
	<p slot="en">He runs quickly.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Han springer snabbt.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

## Irregular and independent adverbs

Some adverbs are not built from an adjective, or differ from the expected
form:

| Adverb | Meaning | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>bra</Sv> | well | also the adjective *good*; <Sv>Han sjunger bra.</Sv> |
| <Sv>väl</Sv> | well | narrower than <Sv>bra</Sv>: <Sv>väl gjort</Sv> |
| <Sv>illa</Sv> | badly | <Sv>Han mår illa.</Sv> |
| <Sv>gärna</Sv> | gladly, willingly | no adjective behind it |
| <Sv>mycket</Sv> | very, much | with adjectives: <Sv>mycket bra</Sv> |

<Sv>Gärna</Sv> is worth learning as a full set, because its
comparison is irregular and extremely frequent in polite speech:

<p lang="sv">gärna → hellre → helst</p>

- <Sv>Jag vill gärna komma.</Sv> — *I would like to come.*
- <Sv>Jag vill hellre stanna hemma.</Sv> — *I would rather stay
  home.*
- <Sv>Jag vill helst åka på fredag.</Sv> — *I would most prefer
  to go on Friday.*

## Comparison

Adverbs compare like adjectives:

<p lang="sv">{snabb.morphology.ett} → {snabb.comparison?.comparative} → {snabb.comparison?.superlative}</p>

The irregular ones follow their own chains:

| Adverb | Comparative | Superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>bra / väl</Sv> | <Sv>bättre</Sv> | <Sv>bäst</Sv> |
| <Sv>gärna</Sv> | <Sv>hellre</Sv> | <Sv>helst</Sv> |
| <Sv>illa</Sv> | <Sv>värre</Sv> | <Sv>värst</Sv> |

<Sv>Sämre</Sv> and <Sv>värre</Sv> both translate as
*worse*, with <Sv>sämre</Sv> tending towards lower quality and
<Sv>värre</Sv> towards greater severity. The boundary is not
sharp, so learn them in phrases. See
[adjective comparison](../adjective-comparison/).

## Place and direction pairs

Swedish keeps position and direction apart with two separate words. German has
nothing this systematic, so the pairs need to be learned as pairs:

| Direction | Position | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>hem</Sv> | <Sv>hemma</Sv> | <Sv>Jag går hem.</Sv> · <Sv>Jag är hemma.</Sv> |
| <Sv>ut</Sv> | <Sv>ute</Sv> | <Sv>Vi går ut.</Sv> · <Sv>Vi är ute.</Sv> |
| <Sv>in</Sv> | <Sv>inne</Sv> | <Sv>Kom in!</Sv> · <Sv>Hon är inne.</Sv> |
| <Sv>upp</Sv> | <Sv>uppe</Sv> | <Sv>Han går upp.</Sv> · <Sv>Han är uppe.</Sv> |
| <Sv>ner</Sv> | <Sv>nere</Sv> | <Sv>Gå ner!</Sv> · <Sv>Hon är nere.</Sv> |
| <Sv>bort</Sv> | <Sv>borta</Sv> | <Sv>Han reste bort.</Sv> · <Sv>Han är borta.</Sv> |
| <Sv>dit</Sv> | <Sv>där</Sv> | <Sv>Vi åker dit.</Sv> · <Sv>Vi är där.</Sv> |

The same split runs through the question words
<Sv>vart</Sv> and <Sv>var</Sv>. See
[question words](../../pronouns-function-words/interrogatives/).

## Where sentence adverbs go

Adverbs such as <Sv>inte</Sv>,
<Sv>alltid</Sv>, <Sv>aldrig</Sv>,
<Sv>ofta</Sv>, <Sv>kanske</Sv>, and
<Sv>redan</Sv> take the same slot as
<Sv>inte</Sv>: after the finite verb in a main clause, before it
in a subordinate clause.

- <Sv>Hon kommer alltid sent.</Sv>
- <Sv>…eftersom hon alltid kommer sent.</Sv>

See [Placement of *inte*](../../sentence-structure/inte-placement/).

## The small attitude words

<Sv>Ju</Sv>, <Sv>nog</Sv>,
<Sv>väl</Sv>, and <Sv>visst</Sv> add attitude
rather than information, much like German
<span lang="de">ja</span>, <span lang="de">wohl</span>, and
<span lang="de">doch</span>:

- <Sv>Det är ju sant.</Sv> — *as we both know*
- <Sv>Han kommer nog.</Sv> — *I expect he will*
- <Sv>Du har väl gjort det?</Sv> — seeking agreement

They resist clean translation and are best picked up from context. German
speakers have an advantage here, because the mechanism is familiar.

<MemoryTip>
Start from the ett-form: if you can say
<Sv><strong>ett snabbt tåg</strong></Sv>, you can say
<Sv><strong>han springer snabbt</strong></Sv>. Then add the two
sets that are not derivable:
<Sv><strong>gärna — hellre — helst</strong></Sv> and
<Sv><strong>hem / hemma</strong></Sv>.
</MemoryTip>

## Common mistakes

<MistakeBox title="Common mistakes">
	- ❌ <Sv>Han springer snabb.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Han springer snabbt.</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Jag är hem.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Jag är hemma.</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Jag går hemma.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Jag går hem.</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>Jag vill mer gärna stanna.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>Jag vill hellre stanna.</Sv>
	- ❌ <Sv>…eftersom hon kommer alltid sent.</Sv>
	  ✅ <Sv>…eftersom hon alltid kommer sent.</Sv>
</MistakeBox>
