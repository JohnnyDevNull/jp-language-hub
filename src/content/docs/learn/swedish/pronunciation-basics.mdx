<KeyTakeaway>
- A doubled consonant is the strongest beginner clue for a **short vowel**,
  while a single consonant often points to a long one; the difference
  distinguishes words: <Sv>glas</Sv> is a drinking glass, <Sv>glass</Sv> is
  ice cream.
- <Sv>g</Sv>, <Sv>k</Sv> and <Sv>sk</Sv> often change their sound before the soft
  vowels <Sv>e, i, y, ä, ö</Sv>, but common exceptions remain.
- Spelling gets you close, but not all the way: the <Sv>u</Sv> and <Sv>y</Sv>
  vowels, the sj- and tj-sounds and the two word accents have to be picked up
  by ear.
</KeyTakeaway>

Swedish pronunciation is not fully predictable from spelling, but a handful of
patterns cover most of what a beginner reads aloud.

## Core rule

> Read the consonants after a stressed vowel first. A doubled consonant usually
> marks a short vowel; one consonant or the end of the word often points to a
> long vowel. Then check whether the vowel that *follows* a <Sv>g</Sv>,
> <Sv>k</Sv> or <Sv>sk</Sv> is hard or soft.

## The alphabet

Swedish has 29 letters. The three extra vowels come **at the end** of the
alphabet, after <Sv>z</Sv>: <Sv>… x, y, z, å, ä, ö</Sv>.

This is not only trivia: dictionaries, indexes, name lists and sorted tables
follow that order, so <Sv>Ångström</Sv> is filed after <Sv>Zetterberg</Sv>, not
next to <Sv>Andersson</Sv>.

## Vowels

Swedish has the vowel letters <Sv>a, e, i, o, u, y, å, ä, ö</Sv>. Several look
familiar to German speakers, but their exact sound and length can differ.

| Letter | Beginner cue |
| --- | --- |
| <Sv>a</Sv> | often more open than German <span lang="de">a</span> |
| <Sv>e</Sv> | long in <Sv>resa</Sv>, short in <Sv>hem</Sv> |
| <Sv>i</Sv> | often close to German <span lang="de">i</span> |
| <Sv>o</Sv> | often closer to German <span lang="de">u</span> when long |
| <Sv>u</Sv> | a rounded Swedish sound; not simply German <span lang="de">u</span> |
| <Sv>y</Sv> | rounded front vowel; keep the lips rounded |
| <Sv>å</Sv> | often close to German <span lang="de">o</span> |
| <Sv>ä</Sv> | often close to German <span lang="de">ä</span> |
| <Sv>ö</Sv> | often close to German <span lang="de">ö</span> |

### Vowel length changes the word

| Long vowel | Short vowel |
| --- | --- |
| <Sv>glas</Sv> — drinking glass | <Sv>glass</Sv> — ice cream |
| <Sv>tak</Sv> — roof | <Sv>tack</Sv> — thanks |
| <Sv>vit</Sv> — white | <Sv>vitt</Sv> — white (<Sv>ett</Sv>-form) |

Consonant doubling is the common spelling signal for a short stressed vowel,
so <Sv>glas</Sv> and <Sv>glass</Sv> differ mainly in vowel length, not in the
s-sound.

<MistakeBox
	incorrect="reading “glass” with the same long a as “glas”"
	correct="glass has a short a; glas has a long one"
	lang="sv"
>
	German spelling does not use consonant doubling this consistently, so it is
	easy to skip. In Swedish it is a strong length signal, and length carries
	meaning.
</MistakeBox>

## Hard and soft vowels

| Vowel type | Letters |
| --- | --- |
| hard vowels | <Sv>a, o, u, å</Sv> |
| soft vowels | <Sv>e, i, y, ä, ö</Sv> |

## Common consonant patterns

| Spelling | Typical beginner rule | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>g</Sv> before hard vowels | pronounced hard | <Sv>gata</Sv> |
| <Sv>g</Sv> before soft vowels | often sounds like Swedish <Sv>j</Sv> | <Sv>gärna</Sv> |
| <Sv>k</Sv> before hard vowels | like German <span lang="de">k</span> | <Sv>karta</Sv> |
| <Sv>k</Sv> before soft vowels | often close to German <span lang="de">ch</span> in <span lang="de">ich</span> | <Sv>kyrka</Sv> |
| <Sv>sk</Sv> before soft vowels | often the Swedish sj-sound | <Sv>sked</Sv> |
| <Sv>tj</Sv>, <Sv>kj</Sv> | often a front "ch-like" sound | <Sv>tjugo</Sv>, <Sv>kjol</Sv> |
| <Sv>sj</Sv> | Swedish sj-sound | <Sv>sju</Sv> |
| <Sv>rs</Sv> | often merges into a retroflex "sh-like" sound | <Sv>första</Sv> |
| <Sv>gn</Sv> | the <Sv>g</Sv> sounds like <Sv>ng</Sv> | <Sv>regn</Sv>, <Sv>lugn</Sv> |
| <Sv>-tion</Sv>, <Sv>-sion</Sv> | often contain an sj-like sound | <Sv>station</Sv> |

In words beginning with <Sv>dj</Sv>, <Sv>gj</Sv>, <Sv>hj</Sv> or <Sv>lj</Sv>,
only the <Sv>j</Sv> is heard clearly.

## Word accent

Swedish distinguishes two word accents — a single-peak accent 1 and a two-peak
accent 2. A few pairs are told apart by nothing else:

| Word | Accent 1 | Accent 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>anden</Sv> | the duck | the spirit |
| <Sv>tomten</Sv> | the plot of land | Father Christmas |

Do not chase this early. Native listeners understand you without it, and it is
learned far more effectively by imitating whole phrases than by studying the
rule. Knowing that it exists is enough to explain why a word you pronounced
"correctly" was still misheard.

## Spoken reductions

- <Sv>de</Sv> is commonly pronounced like <Sv>dom</Sv>.
- <Sv>är</Sv> is commonly pronounced like <Sv>e</Sv>.
- <Sv>någon</Sv> and <Sv>något</Sv> often sound like <Sv>nån</Sv> and
  <Sv>nåt</Sv>.
- The <Sv>g</Sv> in the <Sv>-ig</Sv> and <Sv>-igt</Sv> endings is usually not
  pronounced: <Sv>roligt</Sv> sounds like <Sv>rolit</Sv>.
- <Sv>och</Sv> is often reduced in fast speech, and <Sv>att</Sv> can be reduced
  before an infinitive.

<MemoryTip>
Two questions answer many reading-aloud problems: **is there a doubled
consonant after the stressed vowel** (length), and **is the next vowel soft**
(does <Sv>g</Sv>, <Sv>k</Sv> or <Sv>sk</Sv> change).
</MemoryTip>
