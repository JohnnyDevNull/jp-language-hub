<KeyTakeaway>
- Fluent speech runs on reusable frames, not on isolated words: learn
  <Sv>Jag tror att …</Sv> as one unit, including the <Sv>att</Sv>.
- A frame decides the grammar of what follows it — an <Sv>att</Sv>-clause, a
  fronted clause forcing inversion, or a bare noun after a preposition.
- Situation pages answer "what can I say here?"; function pages answer "which
  construction do I need?"
</KeyTakeaway>

Grammar pages explain why a Swedish sentence is shaped the way it is. These
pages give you the openings that carry a real conversation while you are still
assembling the rest of the sentence. Start with a situation if you need words
for right now; start with a function if you already know the move you want to
make.

## How to use them

Each page lists its patterns as a table with a register note, then compares the
handful of cases where German and English pull in different directions. Learn a
frame with the word that follows it: <Sv>säker på om</Sv>, <Sv>hålla med om</Sv>,
<Sv>ha rätt i</Sv>. The preposition is not optional and cannot be derived.

Two word-order rules do most of the work behind these patterns:
[inversion](../grammar/sentence-structure/inversion/) after a fronted
constituent, and [BIFF](../grammar/sentence-structure/biff/) for the position of
<Sv>inte</Sv> inside the subordinate clause a frame introduces.
