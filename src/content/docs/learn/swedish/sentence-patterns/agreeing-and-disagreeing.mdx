<KeyTakeaway>
- A positive answer to a negative question is <span lang="sv">jo</span>, not <span lang="sv">ja</span> —
  exactly like German <span lang="de">doch</span>, unlike English *yes*.
- Agreement verbs take a preposition: <span lang="sv">hålla med</span> someone,
  <span lang="sv">hålla med om</span> something.
- Short answers repeat the auxiliary with <span lang="sv">det</span>:
  <span lang="sv">Jo, det gör jag.</span>
</KeyTakeaway>

<TagList levels={frontmatter.levels} tags={frontmatter.tags} />

Agreement and disagreement are almost entirely formulaic. The only part that
needs real grammar is the short answer, which reuses the verb of the question.

## Agreeing

| Pattern | What it does | Register |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="sv">Precis!</span> / <span lang="sv">Exakt!</span> | strong agreement with what was just said | spoken |
| <span lang="sv">Just det!</span> | that's right, confirming a fact | spoken |
| <span lang="sv">Det stämmer.</span> | that is correct | neutral |
| <span lang="sv">Jag håller med dig.</span> | I agree with you | neutral |
| <span lang="sv">Det har du rätt i.</span> | you have a point there | neutral, idiomatic |
| <span lang="sv">Jag tycker samma sak.</span> | I hold the same view | neutral |
| <span lang="sv">Absolut.</span> | unreserved agreement | neutral |

## Disagreeing

| Pattern | What it does | Register |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="sv">Jag håller inte med.</span> | plain disagreement | neutral |
| <span lang="sv">Det håller jag inte med om.</span> | disagreement with a *statement* | neutral |
| <span lang="sv">Nja …</span> | hesitation before objecting; softens what follows | spoken |
| <span lang="sv">Det tror jag inte.</span> | I don't think so | neutral |
| <span lang="sv">Jag förstår vad du menar, men …</span> | concede, then object | neutral, polite |
| <span lang="sv">Tvärtom.</span> | on the contrary | neutral, blunt |

<span lang="sv">Nja</span> has no German or English equivalent as a single word. It signals
"not quite" before you have formulated the objection, and it is what makes a
disagreement sound Swedish rather than confrontational.

## Answering a negative question

A negative question — <span lang="sv">Kommer du inte med?</span> — has two different positive
answers depending on the language you come from. Swedish uses <span lang="sv">jo</span> to
contradict the negation and <span lang="sv">nej</span> to confirm it.

| Question | Contradicting it | Confirming it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="sv">Kommer du inte med?</span> | <span lang="sv">Jo, jag kommer.</span> | <span lang="sv">Nej, jag stannar hemma.</span> |
| <span lang="sv">Har du inte läst boken?</span> | <span lang="sv">Jo, det har jag.</span> | <span lang="sv">Nej, det har jag inte.</span> |
| <span lang="sv">Tycker du inte om kaffe?</span> | <span lang="sv">Jo, det gör jag.</span> | <span lang="sv">Nej, det gör jag inte.</span> |

The short answer picks up the finite verb of the question, or <span lang="sv">gör</span> when
the question had no auxiliary, and puts <span lang="sv">det</span> in front of it.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="jo after a negative question">
	<p slot="de">Kommst du nicht mit? – Doch, ich komme.</p>
	<p slot="en">Aren't you coming? – Yes, I am.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Kommer du inte med? – Jo, jag kommer.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German speakers already have this distinction and only need the new word.
English speakers have to learn the distinction itself, because *yes* answers
both question types.

<LanguageComparison title="Conceding a point">
	<p slot="de">Da hast du recht.</p>
	<p slot="en">You're right about that.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Det har du rätt i.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

The Swedish idiom is <span lang="sv">ha rätt i</span> something. Fronting <span lang="sv">det</span> puts
the finite verb second, so the subject follows it.

<LanguageComparison title="Disagreeing">
	<p slot="de">Ich stimme dir nicht zu.</p>
	<p slot="en">I don't agree with you.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Jag håller inte med dig.</p>
</LanguageComparison>
