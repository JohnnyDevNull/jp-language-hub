<KeyTakeaway>
- A positive answer to a negative question is <Sv>jo</Sv>, not <Sv>ja</Sv> —
  exactly like German <span lang="de">doch</span>, unlike English *yes*.
- Agreement verbs take a preposition: <Sv>hålla med</Sv> someone,
  <Sv>hålla med om</Sv> something.
- Short answers repeat the auxiliary with <Sv>det</Sv>:
  <Sv>Jo, det gör jag.</Sv>
</KeyTakeaway>

Agreement and disagreement are almost entirely formulaic. The only part that
needs real grammar is the short answer, which reuses the verb of the question.

## Agreeing

| Pattern | What it does | Register |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>Precis!</Sv> / <Sv>Exakt!</Sv> | strong agreement with what was just said | spoken |
| <Sv>Just det!</Sv> | that's right, confirming a fact | spoken |
| <Sv>Det stämmer.</Sv> | that is correct | neutral |
| <Sv>Jag håller med dig.</Sv> | I agree with you | neutral |
| <Sv>Det har du rätt i.</Sv> | you have a point there | neutral, idiomatic |
| <Sv>Jag tycker samma sak.</Sv> | I hold the same view | neutral |
| <Sv>Absolut.</Sv> | unreserved agreement | neutral |

## Disagreeing

| Pattern | What it does | Register |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>Jag håller inte med.</Sv> | plain disagreement | neutral |
| <Sv>Det håller jag inte med om.</Sv> | disagreement with a *statement* | neutral |
| <Sv>Nja …</Sv> | hesitation before objecting; softens what follows | spoken |
| <Sv>Det tror jag inte.</Sv> | I don't think so | neutral |
| <Sv>Jag förstår vad du menar, men …</Sv> | concede, then object | neutral, polite |
| <Sv>Tvärtom.</Sv> | on the contrary | neutral, blunt |

<Sv>Nja</Sv> has no German or English equivalent as a single word. It signals
"not quite" before you have formulated the objection, and it is what makes a
disagreement sound Swedish rather than confrontational.

## Answering a negative question

A negative question — <Sv>Kommer du inte med?</Sv> — has two different positive
answers depending on the language you come from. Swedish uses <Sv>jo</Sv> to
contradict the negation and <Sv>nej</Sv> to confirm it.

| Question | Contradicting it | Confirming it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Sv>Kommer du inte med?</Sv> | <Sv>Jo, jag kommer.</Sv> | <Sv>Nej, jag stannar hemma.</Sv> |
| <Sv>Har du inte läst boken?</Sv> | <Sv>Jo, det har jag.</Sv> | <Sv>Nej, det har jag inte.</Sv> |
| <Sv>Tycker du inte om kaffe?</Sv> | <Sv>Jo, det gör jag.</Sv> | <Sv>Nej, det gör jag inte.</Sv> |

The short answer picks up the finite verb of the question, or <Sv>gör</Sv> when
the question had no auxiliary, and puts <Sv>det</Sv> in front of it.

## Direct comparison

<LanguageComparison title="jo after a negative question">
	<p slot="de">Kommst du nicht mit? – Doch, ich komme.</p>
	<p slot="en">Aren't you coming? – Yes, I am.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Kommer du inte med? – Jo, jag kommer.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

German speakers already have this distinction and only need the new word.
English speakers have to learn the distinction itself, because *yes* answers
both question types.

<LanguageComparison title="Conceding a point">
	<p slot="de">Da hast du recht.</p>
	<p slot="en">You're right about that.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Det har du rätt i.</p>
</LanguageComparison>

The Swedish idiom is <Sv>ha rätt i</Sv> something. Fronting <Sv>det</Sv> puts
the finite verb second, so the subject follows it.

<LanguageComparison title="Disagreeing">
	<p slot="de">Ich stimme dir nicht zu.</p>
	<p slot="en">I don't agree with you.</p>
	<p slot="sv">Jag håller inte med dig.</p>
</LanguageComparison>
